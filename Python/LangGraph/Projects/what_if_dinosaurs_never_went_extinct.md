# What If Dinosaurs Never Went Extinct?

## Setting the Scene: The K‑Pg Extinction and a Counterfactual Timeline

Around 66 million years ago, a massive asteroid roughly 10 kilometres wide slammed into what is now the Yucatán Peninsula, unleashing energy equivalent to billions of atomic bombs. The impact vaporized rock, lofted a global shroud of dust and sulphates, and triggered wildfires, tsunamis, and a sudden “impact winter” that blocked sunlight for months. Simultaneously, the Deccan Traps in modern‑day India were erupting lava flows that pumped greenhouse gases and aerosols into the atmosphere, exacerbating climate swings. Together, these forces collapsed photosynthesis, crippled food webs, and drove the extinction of about three‑quarters of Earth’s species, including all non‑avian dinosaurs.

In our counterfactual scenario we ask: what if that asteroid had missed Earth—or struck with only a fraction of its force—so that the immediate devastation never occurred? Volcanic activity might still have stressed ecosystems, but without the impact‑induced darkness and rapid cooling, many Cretaceous lineages could have persisted. This single change creates a divergent timeline where dinosaurs continue to evolve alongside mammals, birds, and flowering plants.

We will explore this alternate history from the Late Cretaceous, through the Paleogene and Neogene periods, up to the present day. By tracing how dinosaurian lineages might have adapted to changing climates, continental drift, and emerging competitors, we can examine what ecosystems, cultures, and even technologies could look like in a world where the “terrible lizards” never vanished. This thought experiment grounds speculative evolution in real paleontological evidence, letting us weigh the plausibility of dinosaur survival against the actual fossil record.

## Ecological Niches: Where Would Non‑Avian Dinosaurs Fit in Modern Ecosystems?

If the Cretaceous asteroid had missed Earth, today’s landscapes would host a mix of ancient giants and familiar mammals. To see how they might share the planet, we can start by comparing body‑size distributions. Cretaceous dinosaurs spanned a far broader spectrum: from chicken‑sized theropods weighing less than a kilogram to titanosaurs exceeding 70 tonnes, creating a bimodal peak around mid‑sized herbivores (5–10 t) and another at the extreme top. Modern megafauna, by contrast, is compressed; the largest land mammals—elephants, rhinos, hippos—top out at 6–8 t, and most species fall below one tonne. This shift means that non‑avian dinosaurs would fill size niches that are currently empty, especially the 10–30 t range occupied by nothing alive today.
